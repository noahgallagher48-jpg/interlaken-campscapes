The R100 Field Guide

For Toni's kit: Canon R100, two zooms, and the 50mm f/1.8 · print this page for a hard copy

Back to the residency plan

Your R100 is a modern mirrorless camera, which means one thing worth knowing before anything else: what you see in the viewfinder is the photo you are about to take. If it looks dark in there, the photo will be dark. Adjust until the screen looks right, then shoot. That single habit replaces half of what film photographers had to learn.

Quick reference

  • Green A+ on the dial handles everything. Start there, stay as long as you like.
  • The viewfinder shows the finished photo. Trust it.
  • 18-45 for most things · 55-210 for the waterfront and anything far · the 50 f/1.8 for people and low light.
  • Half-press to focus on a face, then press through.
  • Indoors: put on the 50, raise ISO before you accept blur.

01 · R100 basics

Three settings on the dial cover everything

A+ · the green square, full auto

  • The camera reads the scene and sets everything. Frame, half-press the shutter to focus, press the rest of the way.
  • Half-press first, always: the camera finds the face, locks focus, and beeps. Then shoot. Skipping the half-press is where blurry photos come from.
  • If the built-in flash pops up where flash would ruin the mood (services, candle light), push it back down; the camera will manage without it.

P · program, auto with opinions

  • The camera still picks the settings, but it lets you change things like ISO. This is the grab-and-go mode once you are past full beginner.
  • Use it exactly like A+, and when a photo comes out too dark indoors, raise the ISO (press Q/SET, choose ISO): 800 to 1600 indoors, 3200 if it is really dim. Grainy and sharp beats clean and blurry, every time.

Av · aperture priority, when you want the look

  • You choose the f-number, the camera does the rest. Low f-number = blurred background and more light. High = everything in focus.
  • f/1.8 to f/2.8 for one or two people (needs the 50mm lens). f/8 for a scene where you want everything sharp.
  • One watch-item: if the shutter number in the viewfinder drops below 60, raise the ISO until it climbs back. Below 1/60 of a second, handheld photos blur.

Where the camera focuses, and how to choose

  • Watch the box. When you half-press, the R100 draws a box on what it plans to focus on, and it finds faces by itself. If the box is on your subject's face, shoot with confidence; the box is a promise.
  • If the box lands on the wrong thing (the near person in a group, a fence, a candle), let go, aim the center of the frame at what you actually want, and half-press again. The camera re-decides from what is under your aim.
  • The universal move: focus, hold, recompose. Center your subject, half-press until focus locks, keep holding, then swing to the composition you want and press through. Works in every mode, on every lens.
  • On the 50 at f/1.8, focus is inches deep. The box must be on the eye, not the ear. Recompose by leaning, not stepping, or you will walk out of your own focus.
  • Several faces at once: the camera favors the nearest or largest. If it keeps choosing the wrong person, step so your subject is nearest, or fill more of the frame with them; the box will follow.

The whole exposure story in four lines

Three settings feed light to the photo. Aperture (the f-number): low is a wide-open lens, lots of light, soft background. Shutter speed: fast freezes motion, slow gathers light but blurs anything moving. ISO: the volume knob for the sensor; higher sees in the dark but adds grain. In daylight you never think about any of this. As light drops: raise ISO first, open the aperture second, and keep the shutter at 60 or above.

02 · Your three lenses

Which lens, when

18-45 kit zoomthe everyday lens

Lives on the camera. Wide enough for the chadar, the flag gathering, a whole cabin, the bridge with sky around it, and any room. This is the walking-around-camp lens: if you carry one lens to a program, it is this one. Its weakness is reach and dim light; when a subject is far, switch to the 55-210, and when the light drops, switch to the 50.

55-210 kit zoomthe far lens

The waterfront lens: sailors out on the water, skiers, boats coming in, and the far side of any field. Also the candid lens: from across a program area, people act naturally because they do not notice the camera. Daylight only as a habit; keep the shutter number at or above the zoom number (at 210, aim for 1/250 or faster, raise ISO to get there). Leave it in the bag after dinner.

RF 50mm f/1.8people & low light

The best glass in your bag. It does not zoom; you move instead, and it frames tighter than you expect, so step back. What it gives you: the melted-background portrait look, and the ability to shoot in light the zooms cannot handle: the dining hall in the evening, song session, Havdalah by candlelight, faces by a campfire. When the sun goes down, this lens goes on and stays on.

Make the 50 shine · two recipes

In Av: set f/1.8 to f/2.8, put the focus point on a person's eye, half-press, shoot. The background melts and the wide opening buys you shutter speed in dim rooms. If the shutter number falls under 60, raise ISO. This is your evening-program setting.

In P: set ISO to 800-1600 indoors and let the camera do the rest. On a lens this fast, P keeps the shutter usable in low light on its own; it is the fastest route to a sharp indoor face when there is no time to think.

03 · Use cases

The camp on your camera

  • Walking the grounds: 18-45, A+ or P. Buildings, spaces, the wide life of camp. Shoot more than feels necessary; the wides are what reports and letters always need.
  • Waterfront and sports: 55-210, daylight. Sailors, skiers, the far end of the action, and unposed faces from a distance.
  • Meals, evening programs, services: the 50, flash down, ISO up. The warm indoor life of camp that phone cameras and kit zooms turn to mush.
  • People you want to honor: the 50 at f/2, outdoors in open shade or late light. Step back, focus on the eye. This is the portrait recipe, and it is the same one behind the frames made of you this week.
  • The one habit over all of it: get close, then three steps closer; shoot at eye level; keep the light on faces, never behind them; wipe the lens on something soft first.
